Shield

How your shared connection is made to look like the phone's own.

Leave this at 64. It makes shared data look like it came from your phone.Enter a number from 1 to 255.
What it does

Every piece of data sent over the internet carries a small counter that goes down by one each time it passes through a device. Data from a laptop on your hotspot passes through your phone first, so it reaches your carrier with a different number than data from the phone itself.

Some carriers check this number to find out you're sharing your connection, then slow it down or charge you extra.

The shield sets the same number on everything that leaves your phone, so your carrier can't tell shared data apart from the phone's own. Keep 64: it's what Android phones normally use. Only change it if you know your carrier expects something else.

TTL engine
NFQUEUE works on every phone. eBPF is faster but new.
What it does

This chooses how the shield changes the number described above on every piece of data.

NFQUEUE (default) hands each piece of data to the shield's own program, which changes it and hands it back. It works on every phone, but all that passing back and forth costs some speed and battery when you share a fast connection.

eBPF asks Android's core (the kernel) to make the change itself, with no passing back and forth. It's faster and easier on the battery, but it's new: some phones don't allow it. If yours doesn't, the shield switches back to NFQUEUE on its own and says so here, so your connection stays protected either way.

Try eBPF if your hotspot feels slower with the shield on, or the phone gets warm while sharing.

Hides which websites your hotspot devices visit from your carrier's filters.
What it does

When a device opens a website, the very first message it sends says the website's name, for example “youtube.com”. Carriers can read that name to spot hotspot use, or to slow down certain sites such as video.

With this on, the shield breaks that first message into two pieces, so the full name never shows up in one piece. The website still gets the whole message and works as normal.

Only the start of each connection is touched, so videos and downloads keep their full speed. Leave it on unless a website stops loading.

Where to split
Leave this on “In the name” unless a website has problems.
What it does

This chooses where the first message gets broken in two.

In the name (recommended) breaks it right in the middle of the website's name, which hides the name best.

Fixed byte always breaks it at the same spot, set by the number below. Only try this if some website or app stops working with the recommended choice.

Only a backup. Leave it at 2.Enter a number from 1 to 99.
What it does

The spot where the first message is broken, counted in characters from its start.

With “Fixed byte” this spot is always used. With “In the name” it's only a backup, for the rare times the website's name can't be found in the message.

Leave it at 2. Small numbers (1 to 5) work best. You only need to change it when troubleshooting.

Off by default. Turn on only if websites are still slowed down.
What it does

Many apps and browsers, such as YouTube and Chrome, also use a newer way of connecting called HTTP/3 (or QUIC). The shield can't break up its first message, so your carrier can still read the website's name in it.

Turning this on blocks that newer way. Apps notice right away and switch back to the regular way, which the shield can hide. This applies to the phone's own apps too.

The catch: HTTP/3 is often a little faster on mobile data, and a few apps or games may not work properly without it. So keep it off, and turn it on only if your carrier still slows down sites like YouTube with splitting on.

DNS

How devices on your hotspot find websites.

Keeps the websites you visit private from your carrier.
What it does

Before opening a website, a device first asks “Where is example.com?”. This lookup is called DNS. Normally it's sent unprotected, so your carrier can see every website you visit and can even change the answers.

With this on, the shield scrambles these lookups (encryption) and sends them to the company you pick below, so your carrier can't read or change them. It also remembers recent answers, so websites you visit again open faster.

If that company can't be reached, lookups are sent unprotected for a while so the internet keeps working, and this page warns you. When this is off, all lookups are sent unprotected. Leave it on.

The company that answers the lookups. Any of them is fine.
What it does

Cloudflare: fast, doesn't block anything. Quad9: blocks known dangerous websites, such as scams and viruses. Google: doesn't block anything. AdGuard: blocks most ads and trackers, but can break a few websites.

If websites stop loading, try a different one.

Network

Speed, lag and battery while you share.

Keeps games and calls smooth while someone downloads.
What it does

When one device downloads or uploads something big, it can hog the connection, and everything else (video calls, games, websites) starts to lag.

With this on, the shield shares the connection fairly between everything using the hotspot, so nothing has to wait in line behind a big download. It only does this while the hotspot is on. Leave it on.

Stops lag spikes on the hotspot. Uses a little more battery.
What it does

To save battery, your phone's Wi-Fi takes tiny naps between messages. For devices on your hotspot this causes sudden lag spikes, which you notice most in games and calls.

With this on, Wi-Fi stays fully awake while the hotspot is on, and goes back to normal when you turn the hotspot off. It uses a little more battery while you share.

Makes the phone's own apps a little more responsive.
What it does

Changes a few hidden phone settings so data is sent right away instead of waiting to collect more, and connections don't slow down after a pause.

This only affects apps on the phone itself, not the devices on your hotspot. It's safe to leave on. Your phone's original settings come back when you turn it off.
